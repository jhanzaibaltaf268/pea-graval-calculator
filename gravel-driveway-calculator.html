How to Estimate a Gravel Driveway

The calculator multiplies driveway area (length × width) by depth to get cubic feet, converts to cubic yards, then multiplies by your material's density to estimate weight in tons.

cubic yards = (length × width × depth) ÷ 27
tons = cubic yards × material density

Worked example: a 50 ft × 12 ft driveway at 4 inches deep is 600 sq ft × 0.33 ft = 200 cubic feet, or about 7.4 cubic yards — roughly 11.1 tons of crushed stone. Add 5–10% for compaction and uneven subgrade, and for long driveways order in bulk by the ton — most suppliers deliver by dump truck and price per ton rather than per bag. For a dollar estimate, run your tons through the gravel cost calculator.

Recommended Driveway Layers

A driveway built to last isn't one layer of gravel — it's usually three, each doing a different job. Skipping straight to a single top layer is the most common reason gravel driveways rut, pothole, and sink into the ground within a year or two.

LayerMaterialDepthPurpose
SubbaseCrushed stone or road base4–6 inDrains water away from the subgrade and creates a stable foundation
Base course#57 crushed stone (3/4"–1")2–4 inCompacts into a load-bearing layer that resists rutting under vehicle weight
Surface coursePea gravel or #411 limestone screenings1–2 inFinished, walkable/drivable top layer

A full three-layer build runs 7–12 inches total. Many light-use driveways combine the subbase and base course into one 4–6 inch crushed stone layer (what the calculator above assumes by default) topped with a thin decorative surface course.

Driveway Depth Guide

Depth needs scale with how much weight the driveway carries. A light-use path to a shed or garden gate can get by with 4 inches total. A standard car driveway wants the full subbase-plus-base combination — 6–8 inches compacted. Anything carrying trucks, RVs, or regular heavy loads should go to 8–12 inches, ideally with a geotextile fabric under the subbase to stop the gravel from working its way into soft soil over time. Best Gravel Types for Driveways

Most gravel driveways use two visible layers on top of the compacted subgrade. The structural layer is crushed stone or road base (crush and run) — angular, machine-broken material that locks together when compacted, giving the driveway structure and drainage. A thinner 1–2 inch decorative top layer, often pea gravel or a colored landscape gravel, sits on top for looks and easier raking. The material dropdown above switches density between these options so your tonnage stays accurate whichever you're ordering. See the types of gravel guide for a full size and use comparison, or the pea gravel calculator if you've already settled on pea gravel as your surface course.

Cost Breakdown

Crushed stone and road base for the structural layers typically run $25–$50 per ton; decorative surface materials like pea gravel or colored gravel run $40–$85 per ton. A 600 sq ft single-car driveway at a combined 6-inch depth (base + surface) lands around 11–12 tons total, so a rough all-in material cost is $350–$700 before delivery. Delivery is usually billed separately by the load, and most suppliers set a minimum order (often 2–5 tons) before dispatching a truck. Frequently asked questions

How deep should gravel be on a driveway?

A typical gravel driveway uses 4–6 inches of base material (crushed stone or road base), sometimes with a thinner 1–2 inch decorative top layer.

How many tons of gravel do I need for a driveway?

It depends on your dimensions. A 50 ft × 12 ft driveway at 4 inches deep needs roughly 8.2 cubic yards, or about 12 tons of crushed stone. Use the calculator above for your exact numbers.

What's the best gravel for a driveway base?

Crushed stone or road base (crush and run) compacts well and is the standard structural layer. Pea gravel or decorative gravel is often added as a thinner top layer for looks.

Do I need more than one layer of gravel?

For anything more than a short, light-use path, yes — a compacted crushed stone or road base layer gives the driveway structure, and a thinner decorative top layer (pea gravel or landscape gravel) sits on top. Skipping the base layer is the most common reason gravel driveways rut and sink.

What's the difference between subbase, base course and surface course?

Subbase is the bottom layer of larger crushed stone or road base that drains water and stabilizes the ground. Base course sits above it — typically #57 crushed stone — and compacts into the load-bearing layer. Surface course is the thin, finished top layer, often pea gravel or fine limestone screenings, that you actually see and walk on.
